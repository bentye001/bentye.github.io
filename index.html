Final year major project · UEA · 2025/26

Tuning a £2 sensor with the structure printed around it

Diabetic neuropathy removes the warning. Patients cannot feel the sustained pressure that turns into a foot ulcer, and the systems that could tell them are laboratory-bound or cost thousands. I asked whether an ordinary force-sensitive resistor could be made good enough by changing the 3D printed structure sitting on top of it.

01Design

Start from a real foot, not a generic last

I captured my own plantar surface with LiDAR photogrammetry, then rebuilt the mesh in Blender — Fusion 360 fought the organic geometry, and the mid-plane of the sole needed the most repair.

Reference measurements verified scale before export, and the cleaned mesh went back into Fusion as a cutting body. The result conforms to actual anatomy, which matters: load has to reach the sensors the way it would in a real shoe.

02Manufacture

Two parts, so one variable moves at a time

The platform prints as two pieces in TPU 90A that slot together: a base matched to the foot, and a removable sensing plate carrying the eight FSRs in open cavities. Nothing is printed over a sensor, so none of them start pre-loaded.

That split is what made the study workable. A failed FSR just means separating the two halves and swapping the sensor — no reprinting at all. Testing a different contact geometry means reprinting only the top plate, so one variable moves while the base, the fit and the sensor positions stay exactly as they were. Velcro straps hold the assembly to the foot, and it can be taken off and refitted without disturbing sensor position.

03Electronics

Eight sensors, one ADC channel

Each FSR sits in a voltage divider against a 10 kΩ fixed resistor, picked off the datasheet curves as the best compromise between low-load sensitivity and not saturating early. A 74HC4051 multiplexer lets all eight share a single ADC input on an ESP32-S3. I proved the circuit on breadboard before committing to the PCB.

The board sits in its own enclosure strapped at the ankle rather than underfoot, with the sensor loom routed down to the plate and the whole system powered over USB-C. Keeping the electronics off the foot means nothing rigid sits in the load path, which would otherwise corrupt the very measurement the device exists to take.

04Calibration

Known masses, controlled loading

A custom jig applies vertical load to a printed test cube containing an embedded sensor, in 2.5 kg steps up to 12.5 kg — roughly 280 kPa once converted through the known contact area. Three contact geometries (flat dome, dome and puck) were characterised across four infill densities.

Output was sampled at 200 Hz and averaged over five seconds per step to settle the signal. Third-order polynomials fitted the non-linear response, the dome geometry reaching R² = 0.998.

05Results

Geometry changes where the load goes

Standing trials showed the puck concentrating load at the rear and forefoot while the dome spread it more evenly — geometry altering distribution, not just magnitude.

Walking trials resolved heel strike, mid-stance and toe-off as distinct events, with centre-of-pressure paths tracking heel to toe in the shape you would expect from a healthy gait.

What it showed

  • Contact geometry changes the shape of the FSR response; infill density mostly changes its magnitude. The dome gave the best balance of activation threshold, sensitivity and saturation, so it went into the full-scale build.
  • Step-to-step repeatability landed between 16% and 36% CV, against roughly 25% for commercial systems — some regions comparable, others not yet.
  • The calibration did not transfer. Polynomial fits that worked on the test cubes produced physically impossible pressures once the sensors were embedded in the full footwear platform, so the final analysis is comparative rather than absolute. Fixing that is the first thing I would do next.

Pair project · Embedded sensing & computer vision

Smart Pillbox System

Pill organisers and phone alarms remind you. None of them know whether you actually took anything. This prototype tries to verify removal instead of trusting it, using a camera to count what is left and a load cell to notice what left.

01Build

Event-driven, not always watching

A latch designed in Fusion 360 trips a GPIO button when the lid closes, and that single event starts the whole measurement sequence — capture, weigh, decide. Nothing polls continuously.

The load cell reads through an HX711, zeroed at startup with a settling delay and ten averaged samples per reading, because a lid being pressed shut is exactly the kind of thing that spikes a load cell.

02Vision

Counting pills on a Raspberry Pi

I trained a YOLOv8-nano detector on 2,000+ labelled images across 80 epochs, then exported it to NCNN so inference runs on the Pi itself rather than in the cloud — a medical device should not need a network to work.

Inference runs at 320 px with a 0.6 confidence floor to suppress weak predictions, and the bounding box count becomes the pill count feeding the decision logic.

03Evaluation

Both failure directions matter

A false positive suggests pills remain after they were taken; a missed detection implies a dose was taken when it was not. Precision and recall are not interchangeable here, so both were tracked throughout training.

Every trial was logged to CSV — baseline, updated reading, difference, decision — so the logic could be audited afterwards rather than simply trusted.

What it showed

  • 90% precision and 83.6% recall, strong enough for pill counting, though mAP50-95 of 57% showed the model was better at finding pills than at localising their edges precisely.
  • Real-time sensor fusion was beyond the Pi 3. The camera and load cell were validated as independent subsystems, and the honest conclusion was that agreement between them should raise confidence rather than force a decision.
  • The system can confirm that medication left the box. It cannot confirm anyone swallowed it, and we said so.

01Assembly

Three parts that have to stay coincident

The fork, single eye and pin were modelled separately and constrained as an assembly, so the joint articulates in the model and mating features move together when a dimension changes upstream rather than quietly drifting apart.

The front sheet carries balloon references to a parts list naming every component and its material, so the set can be read without opening the model.

02Detailing

Saying which surfaces actually matter

Each part is fully and accurately geometrically dimensioned and toleranced, so the drawing itself states which surfaces control the fit and how far each feature is allowed to vary.

Click the sheet to read the scheme in full, on the drawing where it belongs.

03Analysis

Checking the sections before trusting them

The section sizes came first, from the standard design equations for a knuckle joint: double shear on the pin, bearing on the fork and eye, direct tension on the rods, each against an allowable derived from the yield strength.

The assembly was then loaded with opposing 35 kN axial forces against a fixed constraint, meshed with parabolic elements and refined adaptively on von Mises stress, so the mesh gets finer where the answer is changing fastest.

Peak stress is 149 MPa and it lands in the rod sections, not at the pin or the eye, so the joint itself is not the weak point. That is worth knowing before adding material anywhere.

What it showed

  • A minimum safety factor of 2.79 against a 415 MPa yield, with total displacement peaking at 0.068 mm, so the sections as drawn hold with margin rather than needing to be grown.
  • Load concentrates in the rods rather than at the pin, so stiffening the joint would have solved a problem that was not there.
  • The tolerancing is what I would change. A ±0.25 sits on nearly every dimension; in production that becomes a general tolerance note with explicit callouts only where function demands them. The pin and bore are both ⌀20 nominal, so no clearance is designed in, and a real part needs the fit specifying properly.
