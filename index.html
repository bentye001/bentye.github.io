Final year major project · UEA · 2025/26

Instrumented footwear platform

Diabetic neuropathy can leave patients unable to feel the sustained pressure that leads to foot ulcers, and the systems that measure it are usually laboratory based or expensive. The project asked whether an ordinary force-sensitive resistor could be made good enough by changing the 3D printed structure on top of it.

01Design

Geometry from a scan of my own foot

I captured my own plantar surface with LiDAR photogrammetry and rebuilt the mesh in Blender, since Fusion 360 struggled with the organic geometry. The mid-plane of the sole needed the most repair.

Reference measurements confirmed the scale before export, and the cleaned mesh went back into Fusion as a cutting body. The platform follows the real foot, so load reaches the sensors the way it would in a shoe.

02Manufacture

A two-part print with a removable sensing plate

The platform prints as two pieces in TPU 90A that slot together: a base matched to the foot, and a removable sensing plate carrying the eight FSRs in open cavities. Nothing is printed over a sensor, so none of them start pre-loaded.

That split made the study workable. A failed FSR means separating the halves and swapping the sensor, with no reprinting. A new contact geometry means reprinting only the top plate, so one variable changes while the base, the fit and the sensor positions stay fixed.

Velcro straps hold the assembly to the foot, and it comes off and refits without disturbing the sensors.

03Electronics

Eight sensors multiplexed to one ADC channel

Each FSR sits in a voltage divider against a 10 kΩ resistor, chosen from the datasheet curves to balance low-load sensitivity against early saturation. A 74HC4051 multiplexer lets all eight share one ADC input on an ESP32-S3, and I proved the circuit on breadboard before committing to the PCB.

The board sits in its own enclosure at the ankle rather than underfoot, with the sensor loom routed down to the plate and power over USB-C. Keeping the electronics off the foot keeps anything rigid out of the load path, where it would corrupt the measurement.

04Calibration

Calibration against known loads

A custom jig applies vertical load to a printed test cube containing an embedded sensor, in 2.5 kg steps up to 12.5 kg, roughly 280 kPa over the known contact area. Three contact geometries (flat dome, dome and puck) were characterised across four infill densities.

Output was sampled at 200 Hz and averaged over five seconds per step to settle the signal. Third-order polynomials fitted the non-linear response, the dome geometry reaching R² = 0.998.

05Results

Standing and walking trials

Standing trials showed the puck concentrating load at the rear and forefoot while the dome spread it more evenly — geometry altering distribution, not just magnitude.

Walking trials resolved heel strike, mid-stance and toe-off as distinct events, with centre-of-pressure paths tracking heel to toe in the shape you would expect from a healthy gait.

What it showed

  • Contact geometry changes the shape of the FSR response; infill density mostly changes its magnitude. The dome gave the best balance of activation threshold, sensitivity and saturation, so it went into the full-scale build.
  • Step-to-step repeatability landed between 16% and 36% CV, against roughly 25% for commercial systems — some regions comparable, others not yet.
  • The calibration did not transfer. Polynomial fits that worked on the test cubes produced physically impossible pressures once the sensors were embedded in the full footwear platform, so the final analysis is comparative rather than absolute. Fixing that is the first thing I would do next.

Pair project · Embedded sensing & computer vision

Smart Pillbox System

Pill organisers and reminder apps prompt a dose but cannot tell whether it was taken. This prototype checks for removal instead, using a camera to count the pills that remain and a load cell to detect the change in weight.

01Build

A lid latch that triggers each reading

A latch designed in Fusion 360 trips a GPIO button when the lid closes, and that single event starts the measurement sequence of capture, weigh and decide, so nothing needs to poll continuously.

The load cell reads through an HX711, zeroed at startup with a settling delay and ten averaged samples per reading, since closing the lid can spike the reading.

02Vision

On-device pill detection

I trained a YOLOv8-nano detector on 2,000+ labelled images across 80 epochs, then exported it to NCNN so inference runs on the Pi itself rather than in the cloud.

Inference runs at 320 px with a 0.6 confidence floor to suppress weak predictions, and the bounding box count becomes the pill count feeding the decision logic.

03Evaluation

Tracking precision and recall

A false positive suggests pills remain after they were taken; a missed detection implies a dose was taken when it was not. Precision and recall are not interchangeable here, so both were tracked throughout training.

Every trial was logged to CSV — baseline, updated reading, difference, decision — so the logic could be audited afterwards rather than simply trusted.

What it showed

  • 90% precision and 83.6% recall, strong enough for pill counting, though mAP50-95 of 57% showed the model was better at finding pills than at localising their edges precisely.
  • Real-time sensor fusion was beyond the Pi 3. The camera and load cell were validated as independent subsystems, and the conclusion was that agreement between them should raise confidence rather than force a decision.
  • The system can confirm that medication left the box, but not that it was taken, and the report states that limitation.

Knuckle joint: detailing and stress analysis

Three parts sized from standard design equations, then modelled as a constrained assembly, accurately geometrically dimensioned and toleranced across a four sheet set, and loaded in a static stress study to confirm the sections hold. The drawings below carry the full scheme.

01Assembly

A constrained three-part assembly

The fork, single eye and pin were modelled separately and constrained as an assembly, so the joint articulates in the model and mating features stay together when a dimension changes upstream.

The front sheet carries balloon references to a parts list naming every component and its material, so the set can be read without opening the model.

02Detailing

Detail drawings and tolerancing

Each part is fully and accurately geometrically dimensioned and toleranced, so the drawing itself states which surfaces control the fit and how far each feature is allowed to vary.

The full scheme is on the sheet; click it to enlarge.

03Analysis

Sizing and stress analysis

The section sizes came first, from the standard design equations for a knuckle joint: double shear on the pin, bearing on the fork and eye, direct tension on the rods, each against an allowable derived from the yield strength.

The assembly was then loaded with opposing 35 kN axial forces against a fixed constraint, meshed with parabolic elements and refined adaptively on von Mises stress, so the mesh gets finer where the answer is changing fastest.

Peak stress is 149 MPa and it lands in the rod sections, not at the pin or the eye, so the joint itself is not the weak point.

What it showed

  • A minimum safety factor of 2.79 against a 415 MPa yield, with total displacement peaking at 0.068 mm, so the sections as drawn hold with margin rather than needing to be grown.
  • Load concentrates in the rods rather than at the pin, so there is no need to stiffen the joint itself.
  • The tolerancing is what I would change. A ±0.25 sits on nearly every dimension; in production that becomes a general tolerance note with explicit callouts only where function demands them. The pin and bore are both ⌀20 nominal, so no clearance is designed in, and a real part needs the fit specifying properly.
